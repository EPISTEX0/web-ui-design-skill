Salt and Iron

How one stretch of coast boiled seawater into salt, hammered ore into ships, and watched the sea take both back.

On now, 21 March 2026 to 10 January 2027.
Five galleries and twelve objects to look for. Allow about ninety minutes.

1

The pans

1780 to 1860

Until the 1860s the Harwick marshes were crowded with salt pans: shallow trays where brine was boiled down over coal fires. The salt cured the herring that paid for the town’s first stone quay.

  1. 1

    Salt rake

    Date
    c. 1820
    Made of
    Ash and wrought iron
    Catalogue
    HMM 1987.14

    Used to draw the crust of crystals to the edge of the pan. The iron blade is worn into a curve by decades of the same stroke, made a few thousand times a day.

  2. 2

    Lead pan fragment

    Date
    c. 1790
    Made of
    Rolled lead
    Catalogue
    HMM 1962.3

    Lead was cheap and held heat well, but it sagged and leaked. Look at the underside, where repair patches were hammered over repair patches until the pan was more patch than pan.

  3. 3

    Customs ledger

    Date
    1811
    Made of
    Paper, calf leather
    Catalogue
    HMM 1930.1

    Every barrel of salt that left the quay was entered here by weight and buyer. Salt was taxed heavily, which is why the clerk’s handwriting is so careful.

2

The slip

1850 to 1910

In 1851 the Harwick yard launched its first iron-framed hull. Within a generation oak was the exception. This gallery follows the metal from foundry floor to launch ways.

  1. 4

    Riveter’s hammer set

    Date
    c. 1885
    Made of
    Iron and steel
    Catalogue
    HMM 1974.56

    A hull plate was held together by tens of thousands of hot rivets, each closed by a team of four. The set’s dished head left the neat round dome you can still see on old iron ships.

  2. 5

    Iron knee from the brig Alder Rose

    Date
    1857
    Made of
    Wrought iron
    Catalogue
    HMM 1991.2

    A bracket joining deck beam to hull side. Oak knees had to be cut from naturally curved trees; this one was bent in an afternoon. It was recovered when the brig was broken up in 1902.

  3. 6

    Foundry pattern for an anchor

    Date
    c. 1900
    Made of
    Pine, lead weights
    Catalogue
    HMM 2003.8

    The anchor itself was cast in iron, but first it was carved in wood, full size, so a sand mould could be pressed around it. The finished anchor weighed 1.2 tonnes.

3

The quay

1860 to 1940

Salt went out and pig iron came in. For eighty years the quay was the town’s noisiest street, worked by crane drivers, stevedores and tallymen.

  1. 7

    Bill of lading for forty tons of pig iron

    Date
    1902
    Made of
    Printed paper
    Catalogue
    HMM 1958.77

    The paper that moved a cargo: who shipped it, who owned it and where it was bound. This one covers the iron that went into the yard’s last big order, a coastal steamer.

  2. 8

    Stevedore’s hooks

    Date
    c. 1920
    Made of
    Steel, ash
    Catalogue
    HMM 1999.31

    A pair of hand hooks used to drag sacks and crates. Their owner scratched initials into both handles so they would not walk off at the end of a shift.

4

Wrack and rust

Wrecks and what the sea does to iron

Salt is what makes iron rust faster at sea. Chloride settles into the metal and keeps working long after the object is lifted from the water. Here the museum’s conservators show how they stop it.

  1. 9

    Concreted shot from Gull Ledge

    Date
    c. 1760
    Made of
    Iron, marine concretion
    Catalogue
    HMM 2011.4

    Cannonballs fused into a single lump by rust and shell. X-rays show the iron inside has almost entirely turned to graphite; the lump holds together only because the sea built a second skin around it.

  2. 10

    Rudder pintle from the SS Tern

    Date
    1911
    Made of
    Wrought iron
    Catalogue
    HMM 2019.12

    Still in the tank where it is being soaked in fresh alkaline water to draw the salt out. The process takes about three years. Through the window you can see this month’s water change.

5

The light

1874 to 1950

Everything in the exhibition came and went past Harwick Point. The last gallery is the lamp that marked it, and the people who kept it burning.

  1. 11

    Fresnel lens panel

    Date
    1874
    Made of
    Cut glass, brass
    Catalogue
    HMM 1966.9

    One of twenty-four panels that bent a single oil flame into a beam visible from twenty miles. Stand at the marked spot and the gallery lights will pulse the way the beam did.

  2. 12

    Keeper’s log for the winter of 1893

    Date
    1893
    Made of
    Ink on paper
    Catalogue
    HMM 1948.20

    Page after page of weather, in a small steady hand. On the night of 11 December the entry reads, in full: “Gale from the north-east. Lamp kept. Three vessels seen to pass.”

Before you come

Can I get around with a wheelchair or pushchair?

Yes. The ground floor is step-free from the quay entrance and every gallery is on it. A lift serves the café. Wheelchairs and folding stools can be borrowed at the desk.

Are the galleries dark?

Gallery 5 and the log case in Gallery 4 are kept dim to protect paper and glass. Use the Low light button at the top of this page to darken the guide so it is easier on your eyes.

Can I take photographs?

Yes, without flash. Tripods are not allowed in the galleries.

Is there a café?

The Chandlery café is by the entrance and serves hot drinks, soup and cake until 16:30.

Can I touch anything?

Only what is marked with a hand symbol. In Gallery 2 you can handle a replica rivet set and try the hammer.
