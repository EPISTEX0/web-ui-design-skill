Harwick Maritime Museum presents

Salt and Iron

Two materials built Harwick: the salt that preserved the catch, and the iron that carried it to market. Five galleries follow them from the pans to the wreck site.

Five galleries, bow to stern

The exhibition runs through the museum’s Quay Hall in one direction. Choose a section of the hull to see what is in each room.

    Gallery 1 · Entrance hall

    Brine

    Salt came out of the estuary pans by the ton. This room shows how it was raked, dried, weighed and sold, and why a barrel of cod was worth more cured than fresh.

    • Salt-pan rake, ash and ironWorn smooth where a hand held it for forty seasons.
    • Curing barrel with tally marksEach notch counts a catch landed on Harwick quay.
    • Customs weighing scalesBrass pans, still in balance after two centuries.
    Gallery 2 · Quay Hall, north

    Ballast

    Pig iron was cargo on the way out and weight in the hold on the way back. Follow the ore, the forge and the foundry that turned Harwick’s ballast into its anchors.

    • Kedge anchor, 1.4 tonnesRaised from the harbour mouth and left in its crust of sea growth.
    • Pig-iron barsStacked as they were stowed, stamped with a foundry mark.
    • Blacksmith’s swage blockA bench tool for shaping chain links and rings.
    Gallery 3 · Quay Hall, centre

    Rivets

    When hulls turned from oak to plate, the yard changed with them. Hear the noise of the riveting gang and see the plans drawn for the first iron steamers built on the river.

    • Plate section with 214 rivetsCut from a decommissioned coaster. Count them yourself.
    • Riveter’s hearth and tongsRivets were heated to cherry red and driven within seconds.
    • Shipyard drawing, linen-backedHand-inked lines for a 60-metre steam trader.
    Gallery 4 · Conservation lab

    Rust

    Salt water eats iron. Watch conservators desalinate and stabilise finds from the estuary through a glass wall, and learn what survives and what does not.

    • Concretion, openedA lump of sea-bound iron split to reveal the tool inside.
    • Desalination tanksFinds soak for months until the last chloride is drawn out.
    • Before and after pairThe same ship’s fitting as raised, and as conserved.
    Gallery 5 · Upper gallery

    Return

    The estuary today: sea salt harvested again, scrap iron turned to sculpture, and the people who still work the water. A quiet room to sit in before you leave.

    • Commissioned anchor sculptureForged from salvaged Harwick chain.
    • Listening benchRecorded voices of former yard and quay workers.
    • Jar of Harwick sea saltHarvested this year from the restored pans.

    Access

    All five galleries are step-free, with a lift to the upper gallery. Seating is in every room. Assistance dogs are welcome. Ask at the desk for large-print labels.

    Questions

    Do I need to book?

    No. Tickets are sold at the front desk during opening hours. On busy weekends we may ask you to wait a few minutes.

    Can I take photographs?

    Yes, for personal use and without flash. Tripods are not allowed in the galleries.

    Is it suitable for children?

    Yes. Gallery 3 has a hands-on riveting station, and Gallery 4’s glass wall lets younger visitors watch conservators at work.

    Is there a café?

    The quayside café is open during museum hours. Please ask the desk for today’s menu.

    Can I come back with the same ticket?

    Your ticket is valid for one day. Ask at the desk about a year pass.
