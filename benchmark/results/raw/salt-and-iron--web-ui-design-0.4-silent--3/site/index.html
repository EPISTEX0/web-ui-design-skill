Salt and Iron

Two cargoes built the port of Harwick: one that kept food from rotting, and one that rotted everything it touched. Five galleries follow them from the salt pans to the forge to the conservation lab.

About the exhibition

About an hour for a full walk through. Every gallery is on the ground floor and step-free.

Salt
Preserved herring, paid wages and set the price of a voyage.
Iron
Gave ships their anchors, fastenings and guns, and sent them down to rust.

For four centuries the quays at Harwick shipped salt out and iron in, and the town's fortunes rose and fell with the price of both. Salt turned a catch into a cargo. Iron turned timber into a ship that could cross an ocean.

The sea is hard on both. Salt dissolves, iron corrodes, and what survives in a museum store has been fought for. This exhibition brings together objects from the museum's own collection, many shown for the first time, and tells the story from the evaporating pans on the estuary to the conservators who now stop an anchor from turning to dust.

Gallery 1: Salt pans

  • Salt rake

    Oak and iron, about 1790

    Used to draw crystals across the shallow pans. The iron teeth are worn to stubs, thinned by the brine they were pulling.

  • Lead salt-pan plate

    Lead, 18th century

    A section of the boiling pan floor. Salt-works kept lead because it resisted the brine longer than iron could.

  • Estuary salt-tax ledger

    Paper and vellum, 1740s

    Records every load that left the pans, because every load was taxed. Open at the page for a bad winter.

  • Herring barrel

    Oak with iron hoops, about 1850

    Packed in layers of fish and salt, then headed up and sealed. Two of its hoops are replacements, cut by a cooper who left his mark.

  • Curing-house tally sticks

    Hazel, 19th century

    Split in two so buyer and seller each held half. A notch for every hundred fish.

  • Ship's biscuit tin

    Tinned iron, about 1880

    Salt beef, biscuit and little else. This tin is rusted shut, and we have chosen not to open it.

  • Ballast stones from the quay

    Granite and flint, various dates

    Dumped on the foreshore by ships arriving light. Some came from places no Harwick vessel ever sailed to.

  • Pig iron ingot

    Cast iron, 18th century

    Shipped as cargo and as ballast at once. It pays for the trip and keeps the ship upright.

  • Kedge anchor

    Wrought iron, about 1820

    Forged in sections and welded by hand. The weld lines still show where the smith's hammer worked the joins.

  • Cable chain, six links

    Iron, 19th century

    The first chain cable replaced hemp at Harwick in the 1820s. It outlasted hemp, though not forever.

  • Conservator's desalination tank

    Working equipment, present day

    Recovered iron is soaked for months to draw out the salt that would otherwise keep rusting it from the inside.

  • Concretion from the estuary bed

    Iron, shell and sand, date unknown

    A lump of sea-bed crust. An X-ray shows a pistol inside, which the conservators are slowly freeing.

Questions

The things visitors ask most.

How long should I allow?

About an hour for the five galleries. Allow longer if you want to read every object label, or to stay for a talk.

Is it suitable for children?

Yes. Gallery 1 has a hands-on salt-making table, and there is a trail sheet at the front desk. Some objects in gallery 5 are fragile, so please look and don't touch.

Can I take photographs?

Yes, without flash. A few loaned objects are marked and can't be photographed.

Do I need to book?

No, but weekends are busy. Request a time slot and we will confirm by email.

Is there a café?

The Chandlery café on the ground floor serves lunch until 15:30. It has its own entrance, so you can use it without a museum ticket.
