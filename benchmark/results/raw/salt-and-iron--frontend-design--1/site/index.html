Saltand iron

Two things the sea wants back, and the people of Harwick who spent four centuries keeping them.

Exhibition guide, Harwick Maritime Museum

Why these two

Salt preserves a fish and destroys a ship. The same sea does both.

For four centuries Harwick lived on two trades. The salt pans along the estuary cured the catch that fed the coast. The forges on Anchor Row turned out chain, fastenings and anchors for the ships that carried it.

Salt and iron also ruin each other. Left in seawater, iron turns to rust, and salt locks that rust into a hard shell around whatever it covers. Conservators then spend years drawing the salt back out.

This guide follows the exhibition through four galleries. Open each one to see which objects to look for and what to try. Plan about 75 minutes.

The four galleries

Walk them in order. The story runs from the fire, to the shore, to the seabed and back to the lab.

  1. The forge on Anchor Row

    Ground floor, east hall

    Start where the iron started. A working forge corner shows how a smith drew out a bar, folded it and welded the arms of an anchor by hand, one heat at a time.

    Look for the hammer marks. Each is a small record of one blow.

    Objects to look for

    • Smith’s tongs and swage block. Worn smooth where hands held them.
    • Chain-cable link, hand-forged. Compare its welds with the machine-made link beside it.
    • The 1791 anchor. Seen here as it left the forge. It reappears in gallery three.

  2. The salt pans

    Ground floor, west hall

    Seawater went into shallow clay pans and the sun and wind took the rest. This gallery lays out the year of a salt worker, from spring flooding to the autumn rake.

    Touch the sample trays. Fine salt, coarse salt and the grey “bay salt” used for packing fish each feel different.

    Objects to look for

    • Wooden salt rakes and wheelbarrows. Bleached pale by decades of brine.
    • Cod-packing barrels. The stencilled marks record who shipped them.
    • Pan-keeper’s ledger. Salt sold by the bushel, with the weather noted beside each entry.

  3. Lost cargo

    Upper floor, the long gallery

    In the dim light of this gallery the two trades meet. Ships carrying salt and iron went down off the Harwick bar, and divers brought some of it back.

    The raised anchor sits in a case at the centre. Its surface is crust, not metal. The metal is still under there.

    Objects to look for

    • The raised anchor. 176 years on the seabed, 4 minutes in the air before it was wetted again.
    • Concretion block. A lump of rust and shell that once held a ship’s fastenings. An X-ray beside it shows what is inside.
    • Dive log and survey drawings. The wreck as it lay on the seabed.

  4. The conservation lab

    Upper floor, glass-walled studio

    Raised iron is full of salt. If it simply dries, it flakes apart in months. So it is soaked, and the salt is drawn out over years.

    Conservators work in the studio on most days. Ask them what is in the tanks.

    Objects to look for

    • Desalination tanks. The water is tested for salt every week and the readings are posted on the wall.
    • Before and after pairs. The same fastening, crusted and then cleaned.
    • Tools of the trade. Scalpels, air-scribes and a very large amount of cotton wool.
